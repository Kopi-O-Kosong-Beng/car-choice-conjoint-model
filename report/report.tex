\section*{Summary}
Our graded submission, \texttt{cand\_pool5050\_final00.csv}, scored 1.185 on the public
leaderboard (3rd place) and 1.185 on the private one (4th place), against a benchmark
of 1.386. It is an equally weighted log-space pool of two
independently built models, both calibrated to a single constant that we measured from the
leaderboard itself rather than fitted on our folds. The public score and the private score
were identical to three decimal places, consistent with a pipeline that did not overfit
the public board, and every forecast we committed to version control before uploading
came true. This report describes the validation machinery that every decision passed through
(Section~\ref{sec:valid}), the model (Section~\ref{sec:model}), the experiments that
failed and what each failure taught us (Section~\ref{sec:fail}), what the fitted models
say about car buyers (Section~\ref{sec:insight}), and how local scores related to the two
leaderboards (Section~\ref{sec:lb}).

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:data}

Each of 1{,}135 survey respondents faced 19 choice tasks. A task shows three configured
safety bundles and a fourth option, ``none of these'', and the respondent picks one. We
predict the four choice probabilities for every task of 263 \emph{new} respondents
(4{,}997 tasks), scored by mean multiclass log loss. About 70\% of the test rows form the
public leaderboard and the remaining 30\%, roughly 1{,}499 rows, form the private one that
decides grades.

Three facts about the data shaped everything we built. First, the outside option is the
single most chosen alternative: 30.2\% of training choices, against 22.0, 25.0 and 22.7\%
for the three real bundles. Declining to buy is the modal behaviour, not an afterthought.
Second, the 19 product attributes are ordinal tiers (three to seven levels; price has
twelve), so treating levels as numbers imposes a linearity the data rejects
(Section~\ref{sec:insight}). Third, the test respondents are a different population:
markedly wealthier (median income \$60k in training against \$80k in test; mean \$75k
against \$151k), better educated, and far more concentrated in luxury vehicle segments
(69\% of test tasks against 9\% of training tasks). The competition is therefore as much about
surviving a population shift as about fitting choices.

\section{Validation before modelling}\label{sec:valid}

\textbf{Grouped folds.} All cross-validation uses one fixed five-fold split grouped by
respondent. Each person answers 19 tasks; a row-wise split would let a model learn ``this
person is frugal'' from fifteen of their answers and be graded on the other four. Test
respondents are strangers, so the local protocol must make every scored respondent a
stranger too. A model's score on these held-out people is its out-of-fold (OOF) log
loss, the held-out score quoted throughout this report. Blend weights are refit
five times, each time excluding the fold being scored, so no number we acted on had seen
its own tuning data; we call a score produced under that discipline \emph{nested}.

\textbf{Noise floors before margins.} Early in the project we measured how much scores move
when nothing changes but the random seed, because a claimed improvement smaller than that
movement is not a result. The three floors differ by an order of magnitude, and using the
wrong one cost us once (Section~\ref{sec:fail}):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
comparison & correct yardstick & measured \\
\midrule
two single models & seed sd of one model & 0.00283 \\
two blends & seed sd of the blend & 0.00048 \\
one model across folds & fold-to-fold sd & 0.013 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Model comparisons use paired tests with respondent-clustered standard errors: a
genuine 0.005 gain is invisible in headline scores (fold sd 0.013) but resolves clearly
when the two models are differenced on identical rows.

\subsection{Track A: a two-member blend}

Track A rests on three modelling choices, each verified by a paired test.

\emph{Part-worth coding} ($+0.020$, $z=11.8$, our largest single gain). Every attribute
level receives its own utility rather than a constant step per level. The fitted price
response drops steeply at first, then flattens and saturates at the top
(Figure~\ref{fig:price}), a shape linear coding cannot express.

\emph{A listwise training objective} ($+0.006$, $z=4.3$). The competition metric is a
softmax over the four alternatives within a task, so only relative utilities matter. We
train the boosted trees with a custom objective whose gradient is $p-y$ taken within each
four-row choice set, making the training loss identical to the scored loss rather than a
pointwise proxy.

\emph{Discrete heterogeneity} ($+0.013$, $z=3.8$). A three-class latent-class conditional
logit whose class membership is predicted from demographics only. Why this form of
heterogeneity works when richer forms fail is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:insight}.

The two members, a ten-seed bag of the listwise xgboost (out-of-fold 1.13682) and the
latent-class logit with task-position terms (1.13863), are pooled in log space with
weights 0.528 and 0.472 refit inside each fold, giving a nested score of 1.12819. We
report these figures with a caveat we discovered ourselves and describe in
Section~\ref{sec:fail}: an encoding defect inflates them by roughly 0.008, and the honest
nested figure is close to 1.136. Two further nested steps finish the track. The first is
a residual logit, a small correction layer that nudges the blend's log probabilities
using six fitted coefficients on three features (relative price, an outside-option
constant, and a bundle's total feature count minus its best rival's), each also
interacted with a luxury-segment indicator. The second is the probe anchor described next. The residual logit was the sole survivor of roughly 160 candidate
adjustments tested on 30 July: $+0.00300$ on the production folds and $+0.00628$ on an
independent fold structure, the same sign twice but only about $1.7\sigma$ combined. We
shipped it as the best-supported remaining gain, not as a proven one.

\subsection{Track B: a tree and a neural network}

The second track was built independently by a different team member and shares no code
with track A. A listwise xgboost arm augmented with matrix-factorisation taste features
(a rank-3 taste subspace fitted on training choices, then predicted from demographics so
that unseen respondents receive taste estimates cold) is blended geometrically with a
listwise multilayer perceptron. A calibration tower follows: per-segment shifts of the
outside-option probability, a temperature that flattens overconfident predictions for
luxury-segment respondents, a ridge residual logit, and finally a low-degree-of-freedom
binomial GAM (generalised additive model) that supplies an independent probability of choosing
``none'', mixed into the margin at 25\%. The GAM head only moves how often someone buys;
which bundle they would buy is carried through unchanged. Because this track's fold
construction differs from track A's (Section~\ref{sub:pool}), it has no out-of-fold
score comparable to track A's numbers. Its one measured public score is 1.194, before
anchoring, and its anchored variant carried a pre-registered forecast of 1.185, band
1.183 to 1.191, which the pool superseded before it could be tested. Its blended arms
score 1.153 out of fold on the track's own partition (recomputed from the submitted
artifacts; not comparable to track A's differently partitioned numbers).

\subsection{The measured constant \texorpdfstring{$r^{*}$}{r*}}\label{sec:probe}

On 27 July we spent one of our daily submissions on a measurement instead of an attempt,
applying a known leaderboard-probing idea to our setting. The file
\texttt{probe\_alt4.csv} predicts the same vector
$(\tfrac16,\tfrac16,\tfrac16,\tfrac12)$ on every row. For a constant prediction the log
loss is exact algebra in one unknown, the fraction $r$ of scored rows whose true answer is
``none'':
\[
L \;=\; (1-r)\log 6 + r\log 2 \;=\; \log 6 - r\log 3
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
r^{*} \;=\; \frac{\log 6 - 1.499}{\log 3} \;=\; 0.266481.
\]
The board displays three decimals, so the returned 1.499 pins the public none-share to
a band of roughly 0.2660 to 0.2669; we anchored to the displayed value's point solution.
\textbf{This number is measured on the test set, not fitted on our folds}, so we
expected it to transfer more reliably than any fold-fitted correction; the private
result was consistent with that expectation, though it cannot isolate the anchor's
contribution. Both tracks were tilted so their mean predicted
probability of ``none'' ($p_4$) equals $r^{*}$, using a single global odds multiplier that leaves every
within-purchase conditional probability unchanged. Track A moved little ($+0.00104$
expected). Track B, we found during a final audit, had shipped with mean $p_4 = 0.211$
and had never been anchored; correcting it was worth roughly $0.0097$ and accounts for
most of the improvement from 1.193 to 1.185.

\subsection{The pool, and why \texorpdfstring{$w$}{w} was never tuned}\label{sub:pool}

The two tracks use different fold constructions (adjusted Rand index 0.002, effectively
unrelated partitions), so no clean nested estimate for tuning $w$ was available from the
two prediction sets we had; building one would have meant refitting both tracks under a
shared fold structure, which time did not permit. We fixed $w=0.5$, which spends no
selection budget, and relied on a bound rather
than a forecast: writing the pooled loss as
$(1-w)L_A + wL_B + \mathrm{E}[\log Z_w]$ with $Z_w=\sum_{k=1}^{4} A_k^{1-w}B_k^{w}$ taken
over the four alternatives of each row, H\"older's inequality gives $Z_w \le 1$, hence
\[
\mathrm{loss(pool)} \;\le\; (1-w)\,L_A + w\,L_B \;\le\; \max(L_A, L_B)
\]
on every set of rows, including the private 1{,}499.
Pooling only pays if the parents genuinely differ, so we measured that too: the tracks'
within-purchase predictions sit 0.0129 apart on average, 2.3 times the 0.0055 floor
obtained by reseeding a single model, and they disagree on 96.6\% of rows. Two distinct models, not one model
twice. When exactly one submission counts and public rank says little about private rank
(Section~\ref{sec:lb}), a bounded worst case was worth more to us than a marginally
better point forecast.

members are simply the last two standing. Figures are plain out-of-fold log loss; rows involving

\section{What failed, and what it taught us}\label{sec:fail}

More of what this report knows came from experiments that failed than from the three
ideas that worked. Three episodes, in increasing order of cost, and then what they
changed.

\subsection{The seed is a hyperparameter nobody tunes}

With subsampling at 0.8 and a random early-stopping holdout, refitting the identical
xgboost configuration under ten seeds moved the out-of-fold score across a range of
0.0090 (sd 0.00283). That sd is larger than most margins in our experiment log. It
invalidated one accepted result: a monotone price constraint (forcing predicted utility
never to rise with price), adopted for an apparent
$+0.00172$, retested paired across ten seeds at $-0.00034$ with 95\% CI
$[-0.00159, +0.00092]$, winning five seeds of ten. The constraint did nothing; we had
been carrying the same model twice under different names. Averaging over seeds is also
the one improvement that cannot overfit, since it selects nothing; yet at the blend
level it was worth only $+0.00029$, because a blend is itself a variance-reduction
device and its members already absorb most of each other's seed noise. \textbf{Bagging
and blending are substitutes, not complements.}

\subsection{A leak that honoured the fold rule}

One experiment scored 1.0996 when nothing else we had built had broken 1.128: an
apparent leap of 0.03, half again our largest genuine gain, from a single derived
feature: the average residual (observed minus predicted) of the other respondents
who saw the same choice-set design, always computed from other folds. The fold rule was
honoured; the leak was in the baseline. Each reference respondent carried a prediction
from a model trained on folds that included the scored fold, and a deep tree can partially
memorise a design, so subtracting the baseline handed the target its own label back with
the sign flipped. Rebuilt with the leak made structurally impossible (for every ordered
pair of folds, fit excluding both), the gain fell to $-0.00430$ ($z=-4.54$). The clean
version had passed both of our leak detectors, and passed them more convincingly than the
leaky one. A signal test can establish that a feature contains information; it cannot
establish that a flexible learner's gain comes from that information rather than from a
leak riding alongside it. Only a construction that makes the leak impossible settles
the question.

\subsection{A structural leak that survived 47 iterations}

A design-share encoding (per-design empirical choice shares, built with the standard
leave-one-fold-out discipline) sat in the production model for 47 iterations and appeared
to be worth $+0.022$. It was a leak of a kind our checks were not designed to catch: the
encoding function was applied once, before the cross-validation loop, so training rows
were encoded from a reference set that contained the scored fold. Because the defect is
baked into the construction, it appears \emph{uniformly} in every fold; per-fold
consistency, our standard screen, proves nothing about honesty. Three tests do work, and
we used all of them: build an honest twin with matched support (honest value $-0.00596$,
$z=-3.53$); check whether the gain grows with model capacity, since real effects saturate
while leaks do not (from $-0.0004$ at depth 4 to $+0.078$ at depth 10); and ablate the
feature entirely. The retraction also voided our hyperparameter sweep, whose honest
ranking reversed, and explains a period in which local gains produced public
\emph{regressions}: two submissions built on contaminated validation improved locally by
0.005 and 0.016 and scored 1.209 and 1.205 publicly, against 1.199 before them. Five
pre-registered gates had passed for one of those submissions; every gate was computed on
reweightings of the same contaminated out-of-fold predictions. \textbf{Many tests
sharing one assumption are one test.}

\subsection{What the episode changed}

After the discovery, plain cross-validation scores were treated as an anti-signal below
1.128 (past that point, a better local score predicted a worse public one), and
decisions moved to a segment-reweighted out-of-fold metric (training
respondents reweighted to the test segment mix) that tracked the public board to 0.001
when leak-free. Its effective sample is only 208 of 1{,}135 respondents, so we used it to
judge and never to fit; fitting on a reweighted objective was refuted three separate
times in our log, each time because the effective-sample collapse costs more variance
than the bias it removes.

\subsection{Three kinds of buyer, and what the aggregate curve hides}

The latent-class model splits respondents into three near-equal segments (shares rounded
to one decimal), with membership predicted from demographics; the strongest drivers are
annual mileage, night driving, education and parking situation:

This reframes Figure~\ref{fig:price}. A single logit fitted to everyone shows the
flattening curve, which invites a psychophysical reading (people perceive price ratios,
not differences). The segment model suggests much of that flattening is a mixing artefact:
average a steep response, a flat one and a mild one, and the aggregate bends even though
no segment's own curve does. Whichever reading is right, the pricing implication stands:
at the top of the range one price step is nearly invisible to buyers, so shaving a single
tier off a premium bundle buys almost nothing.
One caveat needs stating: a positive price coefficient
for the second segment is not behaviourally standard, and we cannot distinguish price
acting as a quality signal from residual confounding with bundle richness, so we present
it as an estimated pattern rather than established psychology. Commercially the third
segment is the finding: \textbf{nearly a third of respondents belong to a segment inclined to
decline at every price we tested}, so discounting is unlikely to reach them, and the
leverage sits in the first two segments.

\subsection{Heterogeneity is only usable in discrete form}

Mixed logit with continuous random coefficients estimates large heterogeneity (price
sensitivity varying six-fold across the interquartile range, outside-option constant
sd 2.47) yet scored 1.173 against 1.157 for the fixed-coefficient model and earned zero
blend weight. Hierarchical Bayes, the textbook tool for panel conjoint, made the reason
quantitative. Scoring identical rows two ways:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
prediction for the same rows & log loss \\
\midrule
each respondent's own fitted tastes (posterior mean) & 0.352 \\
population-averaged tastes, what a new respondent gets & 1.229 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The 0.877 gap is the cold-start problem stated as a number: everything HB learns about
individuals is structurally unavailable for the 263 strangers it is graded on. Its full
overall score, 1.23703 in the table of Section~\ref{sub:field}, makes it our worst
model. The latent-class model wins with the same idea
because it routes heterogeneity through a bottleneck: demographics predict a three-way
membership ($(3-1)\times 29$ demographics $=58$ parameters) rather than shifting all 73
part-worths per demographic ($6\times 73=438$), so HB reached eight times the parameters
from a fifth of the demographics. Ablating demographics \emph{improved} HB from 1.237 to 1.164 while the same
information carries 93\% of the latent-class model's gain. \textbf{Heterogeneity helps
only to the extent it can be recovered from what you observe about a stranger}, and a
low-dimensional discrete route is what makes that recovery estimable from 1{,}135 people.

\subsection{Opting out is not a separate decision}

Since ``none'' is the modal choice, we tried treating it specially twice: a two-stage
model (buy at all, then which bundle) scored 1.172 ($z=-11$), and a nested logit
isolating the outside option matched plain multinomial logit to three decimals, so the
textbook correction for independence of irrelevant alternatives was not the binding
constraint either. Declining is governed by
the same utility scale as choosing between bundles; what varies across people is the
threshold, which is what the latent-class constants capture.

The walk-away rate rises steadily across the 19 tasks of a survey session
(Figure~\ref{fig:fatigue}), and the data favour one explanation over its rival. Adding task-position terms to the latent-class utility was
worth $+0.0053$ ($z=6.3$) and replicated on an independent fold structure ($+0.0042$,
$z=4.7$). A specification allowing only the price coefficient to steepen with task
position reproduces 105\% of the observed drift in declining, while a pure scale-drift
account (respondents answering more noisily) reproduces essentially none of it. Late in
a session people are not choosing randomly; they are behaving like sharper price
hagglers, and since the outside option is always the cheapest thing on offer, sharper
price response mechanically raises refusals. The effect retains 119\% of its value under
reweighting to the test population, the strongest retention of any gain we measured.

\subsection{The population shift, and a prediction it let us test}

Because test respondents are wealthier, we added explicit price-by-income interactions
(linear models extrapolate; trees cannot) and audited every accepted gain by reweighting
training respondents to the test income distribution: part-worth coding retains 101\% of
its value, the listwise objective 112\%, the latent-class model 79\%. The membership
channel also made a falsifiable claim: routing wealthier respondents toward buying
segments, the latent-class model predicted a test walk-away rate of 0.223, far below the
training rate of 0.302. The probe measured 0.266. The direction was right, wealthier
people do decline less, and the magnitude was overshot, which is why we anchored to the
measured value rather than to any model's opinion.

\subsection{Where the remaining headroom lies}

We can state where the remaining accuracy sits and why we stopped searching for it. An
oracle handed each respondent's own observed choice rates scores 0.986 against 1.130 for
the blend as it stood when we measured this, so under this construction the remaining
loss lives in a 0.144 gap of person-specific behaviour. Decomposing choice variance,
only the outside option is strongly personal (33\% between-respondent, against 5 to 7\%
for the bundles), and demographics as we fitted them explain just 11.2\% of that
between-respondent variation. \textbf{Roughly 89\% of the between-respondent variation
in walking away is unexplained by everything we observed}, and no model fitted on these
features can recover variation they do not carry. Choice-set identity alone also leaves
most of the error in place: a predictor built purely from design identity scores 1.307.
We therefore spent the final week on measurement and variance control rather than new
models; Section~\ref{sec:lb} shows that is where the remaining score was.

\subsection{What the close settled}

The private score equalled the public score to three decimal places, 1.185, although
the private subset's none-choice share alone carries a sampling sd of about 0.011 on
roughly 1{,}499 tasks. Every constant calibrated on public rows, above all $r^{*}$, then
faced rows it had never touched. One rounded aggregate cannot isolate any single
constant's transfer, but the result is what intact transfer predicts: no detectable
deterioration at the board's displayed precision.

The rank still slipped from 3rd to 4th on an unchanged score, and that was predictable
too, because ranking noise and score noise are different quantities. The sd of one team's
absolute score on 1{,}500 rows is about $\pm 0.02$, which tempts the conclusion that
nothing smaller can matter; but all teams are
scored on the same rows, so rank differences are paired, the shared row-difficulty
shocks cancel, and the relevant standard error is 0.006 to 0.012, driven only by where
teams disagree. Our lead over the nearest rival was about one to two paired standard
errors: a probable win, never a safe one. \textbf{The rank moved; the model did not.} We regard
the zero drift, the forecasts landing, and the one-place rank slip as a single
consistent picture of a pipeline that was calibrated, not lucky.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The irreducible noise is large: per-respondent mean loss ranges from 0.59 to 2.71, and
most of what drives the outside option is unobserved (Section~\ref{sec:insight}). Two of
our gains are population-specific and would not survive a new experimental design or
population: anything routed through choice-set identity, and the latent-class membership
channel (79\% retention under income reweighting). The three-segment structure is one of
several that fit comparably; the existence of heterogeneity is robust, the specific
boundaries are not. The shipped tree still contains the defective encoding step of
Section~\ref{sec:fail}: we measured its removal as board-neutral and judged late
rebuilding an unnecessary risk, but a new project should not copy that feature step. Finally, we optimise average log
loss only; no subgroup is separately calibrated, and predictions are least reliable
where support is thinnest: the wealthy luxury segments the test set concentrates on.

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

The code package carries two different guarantees. Fresh training
reconstructs both modelling procedures approximately: track A via
\texttt{model/run\_all.R} followed by the residual logit and anchor, track B via its own
\texttt{run\_all.R}. Multithreaded xgboost and torch make bit-exact retraining
impossible across machines, so retrained tracks land within about $10^{-3}$ of the
scored ones. Separately, a deterministic reconstruction of the graded submission, built
by \texttt{build\_candidates.R} from the two pinned track outputs and the probe algebra,
agrees with the scored file to within $2\times10^{-5}$. Two traps
cost us real time and are documented in the package: xgboost 3.x returns a matrix from
\texttt{predict} where 2.x returned a vector, silently scrambling reshaped predictions;
and in a conditional logit anything constant within a choice set is unidentified, so the
price reference level must sit on a real bundle, with a rank-revealing QR on the
task-demeaned design catching the rest.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}

Three first-week structural choices, part-worth coding, a listwise objective and
demographically routed discrete heterogeneity, produced nearly all the accuracy this
project earned. The weeks that followed added more knowledge than score:
an account of where the remaining accuracy sits, two dissected leaks, and a
demonstration that a leaderboard can serve as a measuring instrument rather than a slot
machine. The submission carried a worst-case bound instead of a bet and landed where its
pre-registered forecast said it would, on both halves of the test set. For future
cohorts, one sentence: measure the noise floor before trusting a gain, build the honest
twin of every clever construction, and when the test set will be strangers, validate as
if every scored respondent were one.
